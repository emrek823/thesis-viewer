**Where does defensible value accrue in clinical AI?** Clinical tasks that drive step changes in care require institutional longitudinal records and real-world outcome measurement that nobody has built yet.
## Overview

Clinical AI evaluation follows a ladder, and each rung measures a different capability. The lower rungs like medical knowledge and patient conversations are where most benchmarks and investment dollars are today. The upper rungs (predicting patient outcomes and simulating disease trajectories) require institutional data that doesn't exist on the open internet. The framework is inspired by [[Sources/Research-Papers/Miller's Pyramid Assessment of Clinical Skills Competence Performance|Miller's Pyramid (1990)]] but adapted for AI vs. human trainees.

These rungs aren't a difficulty gradient per se, but categorically different problems requiring different data and different approaches. At Rung 2, on HealthBench's hardest clinical cases, an agentic RAG system beat raw GPT-5 by engineering better clinical retrieval. The winner wasn't the best model, but the best context engineering. That means the investable edge at lower rungs is domain expertise in clinical retrieval (what data do you pull and when based on domain expertise) and not model scale.

That pattern holds across the ladder. Raw model performance commoditizes fast — almost all frontier models score above 95% on USMLE, and o3 doubled GPT-4o on HealthBench in months. If model performance is a depreciating asset, the durable advantages are context engineering (how and when you retrieve data with the right clinical framing) at lower rungs and access to institutional longitudinal data at upper rungs. Most of the $14B going into healthcare AI right now is betting on the model layer at Rungs 1-3 — which is the layer that commoditizes first.

An important caveat: benchmark performance doesn't equal clinical safety at *any* rung. Models that ace USMLE at 95%+ still make 12-15 severe errors per 100 clinical cases in deployment ([[Sources/Research-Papers/ARISE State of Clinical AI Report 2026|ARISE, 2026]]). The ladder describes what each rung *measures*, not that lower rungs are "solved" — they're solved on benchmarks, not in the real world. This means there's an entire infrastructure layer missing: nobody is independently measuring whether these models actually improve patient outcomes. That's the evaluation gap below.

## What This Means Going Forward

Most healthcare AI investment to-date ([[Sources/Market-Research-PDFs/Bessemer State of Health AI 2026|55% of all health tech funding, $14B across 527 deals]]) is concentrated at Rungs 1-3 (ambient scribes, chatbots, triage, documentation). The defensible positions look different at each level of the ladder, and the capital allocation doesn't match where the durable moats are.

- **Domain expertise and context engineering at lower rungs.** MedAgentBench showed clinical AI agents jumping from 69.7% to 91-98% accuracy through prompt engineering, tool design, and memory alone. DR. INFO beat GPT-5 on HealthBench Hard the same way. The defensible position at lower rungs is clinical workflow integration and domain-specific retrieval, but this requires domain expertise to know exactly what info to pull (when, why, and how). Abridge at $5B pre-money and Ambience at $1B+ are the market pricing this. The moat here is distribution and clinical workflow integration, not the model — but the open question is whether these companies climb the ladder themselves using their war chests to acquire Rung 4-5 capabilities before the model layer commoditizes underneath them (18-24 month window).

- **Data representation, not architecture, is the binding constraint at upper rungs.** [[Sources/LLM-Chats/Claude-Epic Curiosity healthcare foundation model memo|Epic's CoMET]] is a decoder-only autoregressive transformer (architecturally a standard LLM) that reaches 0.925 AUROC on CHF by tokenizing structured medical events (ICD codes, labs, medications) instead of natural language. SMB-Structure ([[Sources/Research-Papers/The Patient is not a Moving Document A World Model Training Paradigm for Longitudinal EHR|Standard Model Bio]]) uses JEPA and captures trajectory dynamics that autoregressive baselines miss. Both work. The real differentiator isn't autoregressive vs. JEPA but whether you're predicting the next word or the next *clinical event*. Natural language token prediction breaks at multi-turn clinical reasoning (Rung 3). Structured medical event prediction works for trajectory modeling (Rung 5). This means the architecture debate is a distraction and the better question is who controls the structured clinical data and how it gets tokenized, because that's the input layer everything else depends on.

- **Data access matters but the scale threshold may be lower than assumed.** Epic runs on 115B events from 118M patients across [1,760+ hospitals](https://www.beckershospitalreview.com/healthcare-information-technology/ehrs/epics-dominance-in-12-numbers/) and also owns the workflow surface area where predictions get consumed (physician views, MyChart, operational dashboards). You don't need Epic-level scale. SMB's published paper validated trajectory prediction across 23K MSK oncology patients and 19K PE patients with open weights on HuggingFace, and their broader platform trains across [[Sources/Portfolio/Standard Model Bio/2026-01-10-technical-positioning-deep-dive|1.2M patients across 15 indications]] — still ~100x smaller than Epic's Cosmos dataset.

## The Evaluation Gap

The [[Sources/Research-Papers/ARISE State of Clinical AI Report 2026|ARISE Report (2026)]] found nearly half of 500+ medical AI studies still use exam-style questions and only 5% use real patient data. 

**There is no [[Sources/Research-Papers/METR Model Evaluation and Threat Research|METR]] for healthcare.**

No independent organization tracking clinical AI against real patient outcomes the way METR tracks AI task-completion with standardized protocols.

This gap gets more valuable, not less, as Rung 5 capabilities democratize. If both proprietary models (Epic CoMET) and open-weights models (SMB-Structure) can do trajectory prediction, health systems need an independent way to know which model actually performs better on *their* patients. The evaluation layer becomes the decision-making infrastructure for every CIO procurement choice in clinical AI.

How this world plays out over the next few years is still evolving, but generally we think about evolution of horizontal 3rd parties (whether a METR like non-profit or investable startups at horizontal layer) or vertical companies own this internally as a method of differentiation.
