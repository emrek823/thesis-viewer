# Healthcare AI is moving towards Real-World Workflow Data and following the work of frontier labs

“Data” is often generically talked about as an important moat in healthcare AI. 
Through our portfolio company [Integral](https://www.useintegral.com/), we’ve had a view from the infrastructure layer of how labs and human-data companies are thinking about post-training data. Outside of a few data modalities (medical imaging, antibody binding data), we haven’t seen many healthcare AI companies whose main business is selling data to frontier labs. But the lens of *“what would a frontier lab actually pay for?*” turned out to be the sharpest forcing function we’ve found for thinking about data moats in healthcare AI. The work labs demand is the similar and often times a higher bar than work application layer companies do when they try to use their own workflow data for training.
Because healthcare is more difficult to verify and a lower veracity domain, companies building in healthcare AI are moving towards real-world workflow data (not just annotations/labels or synthetic environments) for long-horizon and more complex multi-step tasks where the increased performance provides real business value. The “low-hanging fruit” is gone and in this world startups need new tools and talent to make this real-world workflow data a moat. 
## Types of Post-training Data
In our work, we leveraged [Sid Potdar's 3 buckets of post-training human data ](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/data-labeling-other-euphemisms-sid-potdar-txdsc/)- annotations on live model output, from-scratch synthetic environments, and real-world workflow data - to ground us and drive thinking for what this mean’s in healthcare. Similar to Sid, we see the world moving towards real-world workflow data because [verification is more difficult](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law) and it is a “[low-veracity domain](https://seanzcai.substack.com/p/an-ai-bubble-is-a-human-data-problem)”.

Bucket 1 data is table stakes for most companies at this point. The tooling is mature; what varies is whether the underlying task has a measurable answer — prior auth annotations carry ground-truth signal, scribe annotations carry preference. You can ship a product on bucket 1 alone, but you can't always train against it cleanly.
Bucket 2 is hard to operationalize as a standalone business. It works as a sales tactic or a stepping stone to bucket 3 — Michael Wornow, who built [HealthAdminBench](https://som-shahlab.github.io/health-admin-bench-website/) as a Stanford research project, is now founding [Kinetic Systems](https://www.kineticsystems.ai/) to collect real-world workflow data instead.
Healthcare AI is moving to the third bucket because verification is harder here than in domains like coding. [Verifier asymmetry](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law) — how hard it is to break a task into verifiable steps — is high in healthcare because there are no free verifiers the way GitHub provides for coding (does it compile? do tests pass?). Whether a clinical note captured the right diagnosis, or a prior auth decision matched medical necessity, is only knowable from downstream signals: clinician edits, payer adjudications, reimbursement outcomes. Healthcare is also a [low-veracity](https://seanzcai.substack.com/p/an-ai-bubble-is-a-human-data-problem) domain — operational logic is heterogeneous and embedded, with every payer interpreting the same rule differently and every institution running its own escalation paths — so those signals only exist inside deployed workflows.
## How to make it useful and a moat
As we get into how you actually take this data and make it useful, we take lessons from the world of selling refined data to frontier labs. [Integral](https://www.useintegral.com/) has built the activation refinery for this path — a pipeline that runs de-identification, tokenization, and HIPAA expert determination on raw real-world data, then layers signal-preserving transformations shaped to each buyer's spec. Working alongside them has highlighted what actually matters: not the pipeline itself, but the taste to ask the right questions at every step of the stack.
The tech stack for any AI app-layer company collecting real-world workflow data is rapidly evolving and looks roughly the same shape across companies. The domain expertise running underneath it is what shapes raw workflow data into something a frontier lab — or your own training pipeline — will actually use.

Every layer has a question only a domain expert can answer. The questions don't commoditize. That's where the moat lives.
More qualitatively, this has also shifted how we think about “domain-expertise”. Virtue was founded on the belief that domain-specific expertise is essential to building healthcare companies, and we wanted to bring that same expertise as investors. What domain expertise means has shifted over the last 5 years. It used to be primarily about relationships with buyers and knowing how to play the game within a broken system.
Those are still necessary but not sufficient.
Teams now need more expertise in data engineering and the data-quality judgment that comes with it, breaking workflows down into automatable steps, and knowing what "good" looks like for AI outputs in domains like healthcare where outputs are [hard to verify](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law).
## What we see going forward
**1. Training your own model becomes more common for app-layer companies.** Two years ago, fine-tuning your own model was viewed either as a fundraising tactic for claimed differentiation or as a poor allocation of resources, since frontier models would catch up by the time you shipped. [That’s shifting](https://x.com/oneill_c/status/2054604986269802579). App-layer companies are moving off frontier APIs onto specialized models trained against their own interaction data, because the reward signal for being good at any specific workflow lives inside that workflow’s product, and nowhere else.
**2. Standalone data assets become less valuable.** Healthcare is inherently multi-modal. Today, a few data types (namely imaging and bio wet lab data) support standalone businesses and there is a moment in time where labs are investing heavily in these assets, but companies getting off the ground today are preparing for a different future. 
**3. Small, purpose-built models become economically viable for niche use cases.** When the underlying data is already refined and signal-preserved, the cost of building very specific models for very specific purposes — a rare kidney disease affecting 100 patients, a single payer’s prior auth pattern, a niche specialty workflow — drops sharply. You can train smaller, more accurate models surgically because the data engineering is already done, not redone from scratch each time. Frontier-model APIs don’t pencil for these use cases; small specialized models built on activated data do.
