# Healthcare and Life Sciences AI Is Following the Frontier Labs Into Real-World Workflow Data

***Healthcare and life sciences AI is moving from labels and synthetic data to real-world workflow data, whether you license it or capture it inside a deployed product. The moat isn’t collecting it but the domain-expertise to refine it.***
Through our portfolio company [Integral](https://www.useintegral.com/), we’ve had a view from the infrastructure layer of how labs and human-data companies are thinking about post-training data. This **frontier-lab lens** — looking at what labs actually pay for — turned out to be the sharpest forcing function we’ve found for thinking about data moats for startups. It reveals what labs structurally can’t get (private workflow data inside deployed products, exclusive partnerships, etc.), and where startups can build defensibility.
## Three kinds of post-training data — and why healthcare is moving to real-world workflow data
In our work, we drew on [Sid Potdar's 3 buckets of post-training human data](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/data-labeling-other-euphemisms-sid-potdar-txdsc/) — annotations on live model output, from-scratch synthetic environments, and real-world data — to anchor how we think about post-training data in healthcare. 
Similar to Sid, we see the healthcare / life sciences world moving toward real-world data because the [verification problem](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law) is structurally different from coding. Whether a clinical note captured the right diagnosis or a prior auth decision matched medical necessity is only knowable from downstream signals, such as clinician edits, payer adjudications, reimbursement outcomes. The underlying logic is also heterogeneous as each payers interprets the same rules differently, for example. Healthcare is  is what Sean Cai means by “[low-veracity domain](https://seanzcai.substack.com/p/an-ai-bubble-is-a-human-data-problem).” We break down bucket 3 — what we mean by “real-world workflow data” — with healthcare and life sciences specific examples below.

Bucket 1 is table stakes for most companies. The tooling is more mature but what varies is whether the underlying task has a measurable answer. Prior auth annotations carry ground-truth signal while scribe annotations carry preference. More teams are spinning up custom in-house annotation UIs and recruiting experts (or using founder’s expertise) because that’s faster than waiting on SOW from a vendor. 
For Bucket 2, the horizontal infrastructure layer is generalist. Prime Intellect’s environments hub, for example, is what Stanford used to build [Biomni-R0](https://github.com/snap-stanford/Biomni), a biomedical reasoning model. The healthcare-specific players typically use Bucket 2 as a wedge into something more durable. Michael Wornow took the HealthAdminBench playbook (Stanford) into Kinetic Systems, going directly after Bucket 3 with computer-use agents inside payer and provider workflows. Lumos is moving into Bucket 3 by capturing click-by-click traces of expert workflows as training trajectories for agents. 
In bucket 3, **aggregated real-world data** is data that already exists and you purchase from a vendor like Protege or license/contract directly with data originators (wet lab notebooks, as Sid mentions). **Context-graph capture** is data continuously generated by a deployed product instrumented to capture decision traces (much discussed by [Gupta + Garg](https://foundationcapital.com/ideas/context-graphs-ais-trillion-dollar-opportunity) at Foundation Capital and [Jamin Ball](https://cloudedjudgement.substack.com/p/clouded-judgement-121225-long-live) at Altimeter). Building a new system of record in healthcare / life sciences in 2026 is rare — and not a path most startups should pick — but understanding it matters for competitive positioning. The more typical playbook is vertical AI companies deployed on top of systems of record (Dart).
Take scientific lab data as a worked example across all five sub-buckets — this anchors the framework and shows the practical implications when building a company: 
- **Bucket 1.** Scientists look at LLM-generated outputs (literature reviews, hypotheses, methodology drafts) and rate good/bad/why. Post-hoc grading of model output.
- **Bucket 2.** A vendor sits in the lab or the ivory tower and builds a portal or synthetic clone, trying to capture every edge case 50 smart PhDs can think of. Plausible, fabricated, edge-case-bounded. Stanford’s [Biomni-R0](https://github.com/snap-stanford/biomni) is the science version; Prime Intellect’s bounty infrastructure hosts a lot of these.
- **Bucket 3 — aggregated real-world data (no context graph layer).** You go to universities, hospitals, registries and aggregate the data that already exists — wet lab notebooks, claims dumps, imaging corpora. The data is real but it’s *what happened*, not *why it happened*.
- **Bucket 3 — context graph capture (system of record).** [Benchling](https://www.benchling.com/) is the science analog to Canvas — the SoR for biotech R&D. Their recent ["AI Scientist" essay](https://www.benchling.com/blog/ai-scientist-that-deserves-the-name) explicitly positions the structured experimental data, workflows, and decision logs they’ve captured over a decade as the foundation for AI. Same playbook as Canvas in healthcare. Rare here too.
- **Bucket 3 — context graph capture (workflow insertion).** You build a deployed product that observes scientific execution as it happens — the API layer for science, in [Transfyr](https://transfyr.ai/)’s own framing. Reasoning, decision traces, protocol deviations — all captured as a first-class data layer because the product was instrumented to do so. 
You’re starting out with different models to see how well they do on a task you know well. Then you start to hill climb benchmarks or evals that you or others built. Then you go to take the next leap in performance and are thinking about what data you need to be competitive. So then the question for any bucket 3 play becomes: is previously aggregated data — *what happened*, but not *why* — enough to reach the model performance your task needs? Or do you need to deploy something that captures reasoning as it happens?
In practice, aggregated real-world data is enough when the task is bounded and the signal already lives in the existing data — imaging classification, structured prediction from claims, certain biomarker work. Context-graph capture is needed when the task requires reasoning, when the workflow logic varies across customers (payer-specific PA rules, institution-specific escalation paths), or when the verifier itself lives inside a deployed product (a paid claim, an accepted code, a clinician-confirmed diagnosis). Most healthcare and life sciences app-layer companies fall in the second camp.
Whether you then sell that data to a lab, train your own model, or both is a business-model question for another post. This is what we mean by using the frontier-lab lens to understand application-layer defensibility. 
## Raw workflow data is a starting point. Refining it with taste is the work.
Once you’ve captured the context graph, the work isn’t done — the data still has to be refined into something a model can train against. [Integral](https://www.useintegral.com/) has built the activation refinery for this path: a pipeline that runs de-identification, tokenization, and HIPAA expert determination on raw real-world data, then layers signal-preserving transformations shaped to each buyer’s spec.
Working alongside them has highlighted what actually matters: not the pipeline itself, but the taste to ask the right questions at every layer of the stack. The stack itself is rapidly evolving and looks roughly the same shape across companies. What varies — and what compounds — is the domain expertise running underneath.

Every layer has a question only a domain expert can answer. The questions don’t commoditize. The moat is your team’s domain taste at every refinement layer — what gets included, thrown out, weighted, shipped. Same stack across companies. Different output.
## What we see going forward
**1. The application layer is doing more than prompt engineering.** Most app-layer companies aren’t training their own models — but they’re not just calling frontier APIs either. They’re building production loops that turn deployed traces into reward signal, evals tuned to their domain, distillation pipelines, and selective fine-tuning where the data finally warrants it. [That’s the shift Charlie O’Neill describes](https://x.com/oneill_c/status/2054604986269802579) — a deeper engineering stack underneath the product, where the reward signal that lives inside the workflow gets captured, structured, and turned into something a model can learn from.
**2. Multi-modal data and models will win in healthcare, but only with the right workflow corpus.** Healthcare is inherently multi-modal — clinical notes, radiology, pathology, audio, structured labs, time-series telemetry, genomics. Most frontier models today are single-modal or weakly multi-modal, and public corpora don’t align across modalities at the patient level. The app-layer companies capturing multi-modal data *inside the workflow* — where text, image, audio, and structured data are already linked to the same patient, encounter, and decision — have a corpus labs can’t easily reconstruct from outside.
**3. Domain expertise has shifted. **Virtue was founded on the belief that domain-specific expertise is essential to building healthcare companies, and we wanted to bring that same expertise as investors. What domain expertise means has shifted over the last 5 years. It used to be primarily about relationships with buyers and knowing how to play the game within a broken system. Those are still necessary but not sufficient. Teams now need more expertise in data engineering and the data-quality judgment that comes with it, breaking workflows down into automatable steps, and knowing what "good" looks like for AI outputs in domains like healthcare where outputs are [hard to verify](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law).
