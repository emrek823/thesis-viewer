# Healthcare Application-Layer Companies Are Following the Frontier Labs Into Real-World Workflow Data

***In healthcare and life sciences, AI is moving from labels and synthetic data to real-world workflow data. Collecting the data is the easy part — what makes it a moat is the tools and talent to refine it.***
“Data” is often generically talked about as an important moat in AI for healthcare and life sciences.
Through our portfolio company [Integral](https://www.useintegral.com/), we’ve had a view from the infrastructure layer of how labs and human-data companies are thinking about post-training data. The lens of *“what would a frontier lab actually pay for?*” turned out to be the sharpest forcing function we’ve found for thinking about data moats for startups. Looking at what labs pay for reveals what they structurally can't get (private workflow data inside deployed products, exclusive partnerships, etc) and that's where app-layer companies can build defensibility. 
## Three kinds of post-training data — and why healthcare is moving to the most robust one
In our work, we leveraged [Sid Potdar's 3 buckets of post-training human data ](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/data-labeling-other-euphemisms-sid-potdar-txdsc/)- annotations on live model output, from-scratch synthetic environments, and real-world data - to ground us and drive thinking for what this mean’s in healthcare. Similar to Sid, we see the healthcare / life sciences world moving towards real-world data because [verification is more difficult](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law) and it is a “[low-veracity domain](https://seanzcai.substack.com/p/an-ai-bubble-is-a-human-data-problem)”. Bucket 1 is table stakes for most companies at this point. The tooling is more mature but what varies is whether the underlying task has a measurable answer. Prior auth annotations carry ground-truth signal while scribe annotations carry preference. More teams are spinning up custom in-house annotation UIs and recruiting experts (or using founder’s expertise) because that’s faster than waiting on SOW from a vendor. 
For bucket 2, the horizontal infrastructure layer is generalist — Prime Intellect’s environments hub, for example, is what Stanford used to build [Biomni-R0](https://github.com/snap-stanford/Biomni), a biomedical reasoning model — and the healthcare-specific players use Bucket 2 as a wedge into something more durable. Michael Wornow took the HealthAdminBench playbook (Stanford) into Kinetic Systems, going directly after Bucket 3 with computer-use agents inside payer and provider workflows. Lumos is rounding out in both directions: capturing click-by-click traces of expert workflows as training trajectories for agents (Bucket 3), alongside SFT pairs, preference rankings, and RLAIF judges sold to the same frontier-lab buyers (Bucket 1). The synthetic patients are the wedge; the clinician network underneath is what compounds. Bucket 2 in healthcare looks less like a category than a starting point — the durable companies either move forward into deployed-workflow data or expand outward across the full data stack.
Bucket 3 itself splits along an axis that matters more than the others: whether the data is **at rest** or **flowing**.
*At-rest bucket 3* is real-world data that already exists somewhere and gets aggregated, refined, and licensed. Academic wet lab notebooks. Claims-data dumps. EHR exhaust accumulated before any AI was deployed. Economics are publisher-like — license a refined corpus, expand by signing more sources. These support real standalone businesses, but the asset per source is finite, and the moat is contractual exclusivity rather than structural lock-in. This is where data aggregators selling to frontier labs from sources without an AI product attached operate.
*Flowing bucket 3* is real-world data continuously generated by a deployed AI product with a closed loop. Every interaction creates a row; every downstream outcome creates a label. Canvas Medical and Dart Health sit here. Economics are flywheel-like — the asset compounds without new sourcing work, and the reward signal is perpetually fresh.
Our argument from here is specifically about flowing bucket 3 — that's where the verifier asymmetry problem in healthcare gets solved structurally rather than contractually. At-rest bucket 3 remains a real category, especially in domains like academic bench science where no deployed-product layer exists, but it's not the move we expect application-layer healthcare AI companies to make.
Healthcare AI is moving to flowing bucket 3 because verification is harder here than in domains like coding. [Verifier asymmetry](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law) — how hard it is to break a task into verifiable steps — is high in healthcare because there are no free verifiers the way GitHub provides for coding (does it compile? do tests pass?). Whether a clinical note captured the right diagnosis, or a prior auth decision matched medical necessity, is only knowable from downstream signals: clinician edits, payer adjudications, reimbursement outcomes. Healthcare is also a [low-veracity](https://seanzcai.substack.com/p/an-ai-bubble-is-a-human-data-problem) domain — operational logic is heterogeneous and embedded, with every payer interpreting the same rule differently and every institution running its own escalation paths — so those signals only exist inside deployed workflows.
## Raw workflow data is a starting point. Refining it with taste is the work.
As we get into how you actually take this data and make it useful, we take lessons from the world of selling refined data to frontier labs. [Integral](https://www.useintegral.com/) has built the activation refinery for this path — a pipeline that runs de-identification, tokenization, and HIPAA expert determination on raw real-world data, then layers signal-preserving transformations shaped to each buyer's spec. Working alongside them has highlighted what actually matters: not the pipeline itself, but the taste to ask the right questions at every step of the stack.
The tech stack for any AI app-layer company collecting real-world workflow data is rapidly evolving and looks roughly the same shape across companies. The domain expertise running underneath it is what shapes raw workflow data into something a frontier lab — or your own training pipeline — will actually use.

## What we see going forward
**1. The application layer is doing more than prompt engineering.** Two years ago the headline was "fine-tune your own model," but in practice most application-layer companies aren't training models — they're building the engineering surface around frontier APIs. Evals tuned to their domain. RAG over private corpora. Distillation pipelines. Structured outputs and function-calling architectures. Production loops that turn deployed traces into reward signal. Selective fine-tuning where the data finally warrants it. [That's the shift Charlie O'Neill describes](https://x.com/oneill_c/status/2054604986269802579) — not literal frontier-to-custom-model migration, but a deeper engineering stack underneath the product. The reward signal for being good at any specific workflow lives inside that workflow's product, and nowhere else, and capturing it requires far more than a frontier API key and a prompt.
**2. Standalone at-rest data assets become less valuable as ground truth gets baked into deployed-product flywheels.** Today, imaging and bench-science data support standalone licensing businesses because no deployed-product layer captures them yet. As application-layer companies in those domains mature and start generating closed-loop telemetry of their own, frontier labs will increasingly source from the flywheel rather than the static dump. Imaging is closer to this transition than bench science. 
**3. Domain expertise has shifted. **Virtue was founded on the belief that domain-specific expertise is essential to building healthcare companies, and we wanted to bring that same expertise as investors. What domain expertise means has shifted over the last 5 years. It used to be primarily about relationships with buyers and knowing how to play the game within a broken system. Those are still necessary but not sufficient. Teams now need more expertise in data engineering and the data-quality judgment that comes with it, breaking workflows down into automatable steps, and knowing what "good" looks like for AI outputs in domains like healthcare where outputs are [hard to verify](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law).
