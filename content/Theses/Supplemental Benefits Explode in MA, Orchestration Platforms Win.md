## Overview

**Medicare Advantage supplemental benefits are a multi-billion dollar spend with broken distribution.** CMS now permits MA plans to offer groceries, transportation, utilities, OTC, pest control, air conditioning, and dozens of other non-clinical benefits to address social determinants of health. Nearly every MA plan offers OTC benefits as a marketing threshold ("everybody has it, so you got to have it"), and the benefit portfolio is expanding toward food security, housing support, and connected devices. The status quo distribution is remarkably primitive: health plans literally buy $25-$50 gift cards off retail shelves, mail physical prepaid cards with massive breakage expense, or force members to submit paper receipts for 45-day reimbursement. InComm, the dominant card-based vendor, charges "incredibly expensive" administrative fees and limits networks to CVS/Walgreens—whose prices are 40% higher than Walmart. Solutran (acquired by Optum) and TSYS round out the legacy incumbents. Utilization of supplemental benefits is "very low" relative to eligible members. A former payer executive at a national plan confirmed: "The utilization of supplemental benefits and even things like community resources is very low. Like as a portion of those that are eligible, it's very low." The gap between allocated benefit dollars and actual member activation is the core market failure.

**The opportunity is an orchestration layer between health plans, retailers, and community organizations.** Health plans need three capabilities no incumbent fully provides: (1) personalized eligibility matching at the individual level—not blanket $50/month OTC cards but "match every specific individual to their specific SDOH needs" across food, OTC, utilities, and transportation; (2) smart payment rails that restrict purchases to approved categories while minimizing member friction—the OTC call volume problem is real ("the call volume we get because why was my nail clipper covered, is not insignificant"); (3) closed-loop analytics linking benefit utilization to health outcomes, retention, and Star Ratings. Grocers like Albertsons (529 stores, 18 states, $18B+ revenue) are becoming healthcare access points through in-store clinics, telehealth, and pharmacy transformation—but they need technology partners to route plan dollars to eligible members at the POS. The competitive landscape includes referral networks (Unite Us), incentive distributors (Icario, Welltok, Sharecare), meal delivery (Mom's Meals), and companion services (Papa). No single platform connects the payment rail, eligibility engine, retailer network, and outcomes data loop. Plans are increasingly saying they *want* members to use benefits: "It used to be a thing where it's like we offer a bunch of stuff and we don't actually want people to use it. I think those days are kind of quickly winding down."

## Bull Case

**Plans are shifting from marketing-only benefits to outcomes-driven utilization, creating real demand for orchestration.** The historical cynicism—that supplemental benefits exist purely for member acquisition—is changing. A former payer executive who managed Unite Us and evaluated Soda Health confirmed: "We want people to use our benefits...increasingly the stuff we offer is stuff we do want people to sincerely use. And we want them to use it because when we played out all the math on the things we're talking about, we're like, 'Okay, we want it.'" The ROI case is multi-dimensional: medical cost savings, risk adjustment dollars, Star Rating improvement (satisfaction measures are triple-weighted), and retention. Plans that can demonstrate SDOH program effectiveness win state RFPs for Medicaid and differentiate in the increasingly commoditized MA market. [[former-human-resource-executive-at-technology-company-_-soda-f85e36cb]]

**Incumbents are expensive, inflexible, and non-personalized—creating a clear displacement opportunity.** InComm charges high administrative fees, operates on limited retail networks (CVS/Walgreens only), and delivers non-personalized blanket benefits. A former Walmart healthcare strategy director explained: "InComm is a payments platform...Soda is combining the payments platform and being smarter about what the benefits should be and being able to do that at a more personalized level." Walmart built a homegrown "direct-to-spend" solution but it was Walmart-only and couldn't individualize by member. If a plan spends $10M on blanket OTC benefits and could reallocate that more effectively to individualized needs, the savings thesis is straightforward. The air conditioner example is instructive: "If we could have sold them a $75 air-conditioning unit, any plan would have paid for that 100x over" versus a $3,000 ER visit for heat exhaustion. [[former-director-of-healthcare-transformation-strategy-at-wa-425ca871]]

**Retailers are transforming into healthcare access points, creating a massive new network surface.** Albertsons' SVP described a transformation "from simply being a prescription filler to being a true community health partner"—in-store clinics, telehealth, community service centers. They partner with Soda Health, InComm, S3/Solutran, BeneLynk, NationsBenefits, Unite Us, and others across 529 stores in 18 states. They track health improvement rates, digital platform engagement, housing support referrals, and revenue impacts from SDOH programs. Albertsons pays to be a preferred Soda Health partner. The retailer rated Soda Health 8/10, praising "personalized eligibility and data integration" and the ability to "match every specific individual to their specific SDOH needs...across health foods, OTC, utilities, transition." [[senior-vice-president-at-albertsons-companies-_-soda-health-d21da355]]

**The referral-only model (Unite Us) has a structural funding flaw that direct-spend orchestration solves.** Unite Us is the "gold standard" for closed-loop referral networks, but it routes members to community-based organizations funded by grants and government money—not plan dollars. A former Unite Us VP described the fatal flaw: "Alameda County's funding just got cut by 30%. So Unite Us has just put 15 employees full-time on the ground there to service this network and the money is all gone." When grant funding evaporates, the entire referral network collapses. Direct-spend orchestration avoids this dependency by using plan dollars to fund benefits directly through retail and service networks—scalable and not subject to grant cycles. [[former-vice-president-of-network-development-at-unite-us-_-s-d5bf2a8e]]

## Bear Case

**Payers may not actually want to fund direct-spend benefits at scale.** The skeptical view is that plans prefer referring members to free community resources (CBO model) rather than paying for benefits directly. A former Unite Us VP was blunt: "Are there enough benefits out there that the payers themselves want to pay for?...I would say, no, they're not going to pay for something like that." The economics are real—every dollar spent on a supplemental benefit is a dollar off the MLR. Plans at razor-thin margins (UNH MCR at 89.25%) may resist new spend categories. The air conditioner example sounds great in theory but requires actuarial proof at scale. [[former-vice-president-of-network-development-at-unite-us-_-s-d5bf2a8e]]

**Regulatory and political risk can wipe out benefit categories overnight.** Supplemental benefit funding is subject to CMS rule changes and state-level political shifts. The Kentucky example: "Both houses and that legislature changed from blue to red and all of that funding went away immediately." CMS could restrict or redefine which supplemental benefits count, especially as concerns about "kickbacks" and "the perception of how things are spent" grow. A payer executive noted: "I don't think financial incentives are something the government really wants...there's going to be more and more supplemental benefits, but I don't think that there are going to be things that come along with gift cards." If CMS tightens what qualifies as a legitimate supplemental benefit, the TAM shrinks. [[former-human-resource-executive-at-technology-company-_-soda-f85e36cb]]

**Category-restricted payment rails create significant member friction and operational headaches.** The OTC benefit experience shows that restricting what members can buy generates substantial complaint volume and administrative burden. "The call volume we get because why was my nail clipper covered, is not insignificant. So it's a pain when you sort of apply those sort of rails, especially in these populations and can actually sort of backfire." More rails with more restrictions on more benefit categories could multiply this problem, degrading the very member satisfaction that supplemental benefits are supposed to improve. [[former-human-resource-executive-at-technology-company-_-soda-f85e36cb]]

**The market education burden is enormous and ROI remains unproven.** A former Unite Us VP assessed: "It's going to be a royal pain to get this going...a huge uphill climb and a lot of education." The Soda Health team was described as "smart" by multiple experts but also as not fully understanding payer economics: "It's kind of unclear if they really understood our business and our problems...a lack of incorporation of people who really understand how payers work and how we think about our unit economics probably negatively impact how they pitch the value proposition." Without a "coherent business case" showing algebraic ROI, payer procurement teams will stall. Sales cycles in this space run 9-36 months. [[former-human-resource-executive-at-technology-company-_-soda-f85e36cb]]

## Startup Opportunities

**1. Soda Health (Series A-B)**
- Why this follows: Soda is the most-referenced company across all four expert interviews, positioned at the intersection of personalized eligibility, smart payment rails, and retailer networks. They combine what InComm does on payments with individualized benefit matching.
- Wedge: Retailer-first distribution through Albertsons (529 stores, 18 states), Walmart relationships, retailer-agnostic payment network.
- The edge: Personalization engine that matches individual members to specific SDOH needs (food, OTC, utilities, transportation) rather than blanket benefits. Combines payments platform with behavioral intelligence. "Nobody else is doing meaningfully" the marriage of financial rails and individualized benefit allocation.
- Buyers: MA plan medical directors, VP of supplemental benefits, Medicaid managed care plan administrators, large retailers seeking healthcare partnerships.
- Risk: Small company selling to large payers who are "risk averse" to early-stage vendors. $1M annual spend at evaluation-stage payer. Must demonstrate actuarial ROI that no one in the market has proven yet. Optum acquired Solutran, positioning for vertical integration.

**2. Benefit Eligibility and Navigation Platform (Seed-Series A)**
- Why this follows: Health literacy is the primary barrier to utilization. Albertsons' SVP identified "an immense subsection of our patients and shoppers who really don't know the true scope of offerings that's presented to them." BeneLynk does preliminary benefits navigation. A platform that identifies and activates eligible members before they walk into a store or clinic captures value upstream.
- Wedge: Integrate with payer eligibility files, pharmacy data, and retailer loyalty programs to proactively surface benefits to members through digital channels.
- The edge: Proactive identification of high-flyer patients with "highest propensity for adverse recidivism without community support" and automated benefit activation—not waiting for members to self-discover.
- Buyers: MA plans, D-SNP plans, Medicaid MCOs.
- Risk: BeneLynk and NationsBenefits already operate in this space. Payers may build in-house. Member engagement is notoriously difficult in low-income populations.

**3. Retailer-Side SDOH Analytics and Integration Platform (Seed-Series A)**
- Why this follows: Albertsons is tracking health improvement rates, digital engagement, housing support referrals, and revenue impacts from SDOH programs—but across multiple vendors (Soda, InComm, S3, BeneLynk, NationsBenefits, Unite Us). Retailers need a unified data layer to manage multi-vendor SDOH partnerships, report to regulators, integrate with loyalty programs, and optimize which benefit dollars drive the most impact per shopper.
- Wedge: Retailer-first sale (VP operations, not payer procurement) with faster sales cycle. Aggregate analytics across all supplemental benefit vendors at the POS.
- The edge: Retailers are paying to be preferred partners—they are buyers, not just network endpoints. Albertsons confirms they pay Soda Health to be a preferred partner. The data on benefit utilization patterns, shopper health outcomes, and loyalty integration is valuable to both retailers and plans.
- Buyers: Grocery chain VP of operations/pharmacy, health system community benefit officers.
- Risk: Retailers may not pay enough for standalone analytics. Vendor consolidation could simplify the multi-vendor problem before a platform emerges.

## Open Questions

**1. Does personalized supplemental benefit allocation actually reduce total cost of care?**
→ Would validate whether the orchestration platform market is a real outcomes business or remains a marketing/member-acquisition spend. No expert in the source interviews could point to rigorous actuarial proof. If NBER-style studies show supplemental benefits don't bend the cost curve (similar to GLP-1 findings on non-drug healthcare spend), the buyer motivation collapses to Star Ratings and retention—smaller but still real.

**2. Will CMS expand or contract supplemental benefit categories in the next 3 years?**
→ Would validate/invalidate the TAM growth assumption. CMS has been expanding allowable categories since 2019, but the political environment is shifting. If CMS narrows what counts or introduces stricter documentation requirements, the orchestration complexity increases (good for platforms) but the dollar volume may shrink (bad for everyone).

**3. Can any platform achieve sufficient retailer network density to be the default orchestration rail?**
→ Would validate whether this becomes a winner-take-most market or remains fragmented across regional vendor relationships. Albertsons alone works with 6+ vendors in this space. If no platform achieves cross-retailer, cross-payer network effects, the market stays fragmented and low-margin.
