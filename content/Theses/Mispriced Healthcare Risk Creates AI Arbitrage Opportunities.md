## Overview

We've seen with some of our portfolio companies (e.g., WriteWise) that there is an opportunity to arbitrage (and profit from that arbitrage) where there is mispricing between true actuarial risk and what incumbents can't or won't price accurately. WriteWise is an MGU that guarantees total pharmacy costs at a fixed PMPM for self-funded employers, backed by a captive insurance entity. PBMs already offer PMPM guarantees — but they manipulate them through drug exclusions, brand/generic reclassification, and rebate gaming to hit targets on paper while actual plan spend rises. WriteWise's proprietary platform (MavRx) ingests claims data, reprices across a panel of PBMs, and uses predictive GoPricing algorithms to forecast true forward-looking net cost — not historical cost — for each employer group. In one real case, WriteWise identified a $12M+ annual spread between what an employer paid through its incumbent PBM ($27.5M) and the true net cost after rebate passthrough, clinical savings, and alternative sourcing ($15.3M). WriteWise captures that spread by offering the employer a lower, guaranteed PMPM that covers all drugs including specialty, and underwriting the risk itself. The employer gets budget certainty and 10-20% savings. WriteWise profits from the delta between its guaranteed price and predicted actual cost. Both win because the PBM was extracting margin through opacity, not accurate pricing.

We think this pattern — AI-native underwriting exploiting the gap between what incumbents charge and true risk — repeats across healthcare. The structural driver is that 67% of covered workers (131.8M Americans) are now in self-funded or level-funded plans, up from 44% of small firms in 2020. These plans require individual group-level pricing precision, but incumbents still use population-level actuarial tables built for large employers. The stop-loss market alone grew from $32B (2021) to $39B (2024) and is projected to reach $113B by 2034 at 15.1% CAGR. Loss ratios have deteriorated from 78.5% (2017) to 86.0% (2024) — Cigna's stop-loss book hit 90-95% loss ratios in Q4 2024 vs expected 83-88%, and they're "spending two renewal cycles to recover margins." Incumbents are bleeding because they can't price granularly: they either overprice low-risk groups (leaving money on the table) or underprice high-risk groups (eating losses). We're already seeing this in ACO underwriting — Sparx serves 20%+ of ACOs, has unlocked $1B+ in risk-bearing capacity, and enabled 62.1% savings in one risk-bearing contract by providing predictions that ACO leadership trusts enough to take downside risk. And in stop-loss quoting — Gradient AI's SAIL platform processes 170M+ lives and uses "data substitution" to quote groups that incumbents auto-decline because they lack claims history, opening a previously unaddressable small group market.

## What This Means Going Forward

The arbitrage exists because incumbents have a structural inability to price at the group level, and it widens as healthcare shifts to self-funding. Every employer that moves from fully-insured to self-funded or level-funded creates a new pricing surface that incumbents haven't built infrastructure to serve. The wedge for startups is AI that turns unpriceable risk into profitable underwriting — quoting groups with no claims history (Gradient AI), guaranteeing specialty drug costs (WriteWise), or giving providers the actuarial confidence to accept downside risk contracts (Sparx). The buyers are not carriers — they're the TPAs (2,700+ in the US), MGUs, ACOs, and self-funded employers who need accurate pricing and can't get it from incumbents. Gene therapy tail risk ($2-4.5M single treatments, only 3 claims across 2M covered lives 2022-2024) is an emerging extension: treatments like Lenmeldy ($4.25M) create catastrophic exposure that actuarial models can't price because frequency data doesn't exist yet — whoever aggregates this data first builds the moat.

The risk is that incumbents close the gap from above — either through brute-force repricing (Voya raised 21%+) or acquisition of AI capabilities. UnitedHealth has 2,000+ AI engineers and launched Optum Real in October 2025. Nationwide's $1.25B acquisition of Allstate's employer stop-loss business shows willingness to pay for capability rather than build it. If a major carrier acquires a Gradient AI or builds equivalent data infrastructure, the window narrows — startups may have 2-3 years before incumbent AI catches up. There's also a regulatory risk: DOL/HHS/Treasury raised concerns about stop-loss bypassing consumer protections, and New York, California, and Delaware already restrict stop-loss for small employers. Federal action could cap the self-funded growth that drives this thesis. The counter is that the arbitrage is not just a technology gap — it's a business model gap. Incumbents profit from opacity and population-level pricing. An AI-native underwriter that prices at the individual group level and passes savings to employers is structurally misaligned with how carriers make money. That misalignment is what makes the opportunity durable.
