## Overview

We've seen with some of our portfolio companies (e.g., [[Sources/Portfolio/WriteWise/|WriteWise]]) that there is an opportunity to arbitrage (and profit from that arbitrage) where there is mispricing between true actuarial risk and what incumbents can't or won't price accurately.

WriteWise guarantees total pharmacy costs at a fixed PMPM for self-funded employers, backed by a captive insurance entity. PBMs already offer PMPM guarantees but they manipulate them through drug exclusions, brand/generic reclassification, and rebate gaming to hit targets on paper while actual plan spend rises. WriteWise's proprietary platform ([[Sources/Market-Research-PDFs/12-mavrx-platform-saas-pbm-pricing-150a0269|MavRx]]) ingests claims data, reprices across a panel of PBMs, and uses predictive GoPricing algorithms to forecast true forward-looking net cost (not historical cost) for each employer group. WriteWise captures that spread by offering the employer a lower, guaranteed PMPM that covers all drugs including specialty, and underwriting the risk itself. The employer gets budget certainty and 10-20% savings. WriteWise profits from the delta between its guaranteed price and predicted actual cost. Both win because the PBM was extracting margin through opacity, not accurate pricing.

We think this pattern — AI-native underwriting exploiting the gap between what incumbents charge and true risk — repeats across healthcare. Two examples: In ACO underwriting, providers are asked to accept financial risk for patient outcomes but lack the actuarial tools to price that risk. [[Sources/Browser-History/2026-01-27-sparx|Sparx]] built ML models on ACO claims data, now serves 20%+ of ACOs, and has unlocked $1B+ in risk-bearing capacity — in one contract, their predictions gave ACO leadership enough confidence to take downside risk and realize 62.1% savings. The incumbent actuarial consultants were overpricing the risk, and the ACOs were leaving money on the table. In consumer health benefits, the same mispricing exists at the individual level. A healthy 30-year-old on an employer plan pays ~$9,325/year in premiums but uses $2,000-3,000 in actual services, overpaying $6,000-7,000/year to cross-subsidize risk the insurer can't or won't price accurately. Companies building consumer-directed health infrastructure are exploiting this same gap — right-sizing coverage by separating routine care (predictable, budgetable) from catastrophic risk (unpredictable, insurable), which the [[Sources/Research-Papers/RAND Health Insurance Experiment|RAND Health Insurance Experiment]] showed reduces spending 30% with no difference in health outcomes.

## What This Means Going Forward

The arbitrage widens as healthcare shifts to self-funding and consumer-directed models. Every employer that moves from fully-insured to self-funded creates a new pricing surface that incumbents haven't built infrastructure to serve, and every consumer who realizes they're overpaying $6,000-7,000/year for care they don't use creates demand for right-sized alternatives. The wedge for startups is AI that turns unpriceable risk into profitable underwriting — guaranteeing pharmacy costs by repricing across PBM panels ([[Sources/Portfolio/WriteWise/|WriteWise]]), giving providers the actuarial confidence to accept downside risk contracts ([[Sources/Browser-History/2026-01-27-sparx|Sparx]]), quoting groups with no claims history through data substitution ([[Sources/LLM-Chats/2025-12-21-AI-for-stop-loss-insurers|Gradient AI]]), or separating routine and catastrophic coverage so consumers stop cross-subsidizing risk that should be priced individually. The buyers are not carriers — they're the TPAs (2,700+ in the US), MGUs, ACOs, self-funded employers, and increasingly individual consumers who need accurate pricing and can't get it from incumbents. Gene therapy tail risk ($2-4.5M single treatments, only 3 claims across 2M covered lives 2022-2024) is an emerging extension: treatments like Lenmeldy ($4.25M) create catastrophic exposure that actuarial models can't price because frequency data doesn't exist yet — whoever aggregates this data first builds the moat.

The risk is that incumbents close the gap from above — either through brute-force repricing (Voya raised 21%+) or acquisition of AI capabilities. UnitedHealth has 2,000+ AI engineers and launched [[Sources/Browser-History/2026-01-27-optum-real|Optum Real]] in October 2025. [[Sources/Browser-History/2025-12-23-nationwide-to-acquire-allstate-employer-stop-loss|Nationwide's $1.25B acquisition of Allstate's employer stop-loss business]] shows willingness to pay for capability rather than build it. If a major carrier acquires a Gradient AI or builds equivalent data infrastructure, the window narrows. There's also a regulatory risk: DOL/HHS/Treasury raised concerns about stop-loss bypassing consumer protections, and New York, California, and Delaware already restrict stop-loss for small employers. Federal action could cap the self-funded growth that drives this thesis. The counter is that the arbitrage is not just a technology gap — it's a business model gap. Incumbents profit from opacity and population-level pricing. An AI-native underwriter that prices at the individual group level and passes savings to employers — or a consumer-directed platform that separates routine from catastrophic coverage — is structurally misaligned with how carriers make money. That misalignment is what makes the opportunity durable.
