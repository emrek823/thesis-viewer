## Overview

We've seen the opportunity to arbitrage (and profit from that arbitrage) where there is mispricing between true actuarial risk and what incumbents can't or won't price accurately.

WriteWise in our portfolio is one example. WriteWise guarantees total pharmacy costs at a fixed PMPM for self-funded employers, backed by a captive insurance entity. PBMs already offer PMPM guarantees but they manipulate them through drug exclusions, brand/generic reclassification, and rebate gaming to hit targets on paper while actual plan spend rises. WriteWise's proprietary platform ([[Sources/Market-Research-PDFs/12-mavrx-platform-saas-pbm-pricing-150a0269|MavRx]]) ingests claims data, reprices across a panel of PBMs, and uses predictive GoPricing algorithms to forecast true forward-looking net cost (not historical cost) for each employer group. WriteWise captures that spread by offering the employer a lower, guaranteed PMPM that covers all drugs including specialty, and underwriting the risk itself. The employer gets budget certainty and 10-20% savings. WriteWise profits from the delta between its guaranteed price and predicted actual cost. Both win because the PBM was extracting margin through opacity, not accurate pricing.

We think this pattern of AI-native underwriting exploiting the gap between what incumbents charge and true risk repeats across other areas of healthcare. Two examples: 
- In ACO underwriting, providers are asked to accept financial risk for patient outcomes but lack the actuarial tools to price that risk. [[Sources/Browser-History/2026-01-27-sparx|Sparx]] built ML models on ACO claims data, now serves 20%+ of ACOs, and has unlocked $1B+ in risk-bearing capacity. In one contract, their predictions gave ACO leadership enough confidence to take downside risk and realize 62.1% savings. The incumbent actuarial consultants were overpricing the risk, and the ACOs were leaving money on the table. 
- In consumer health benefits, the same mispricing exists at the individual level. A healthy 30-year-old on an employer plan pays ~$9,325/year in premiums but uses $2,000-3,000 in actual services, overpaying $6,000-7,000/year to cross-subsidize risk the insurer can't or won't price accurately. Companies building consumer-directed health infrastructure are exploiting this same gap — right-sizing coverage by separating routine care (predictable, budgetable) from catastrophic risk (unpredictable, insurable), which the [[Sources/Research-Papers/RAND Health Insurance Experiment|RAND Health Insurance Experiment]] showed reduces spending 30% with no difference in health outcomes.

## What This Means Going Forward

The opportunity compounds because healthcare risk is mispriced at every layer (plan-level, provider-level, individual-level) and the startups that win won't be point solutions optimizing one workflow for analytics but underwriting platforms that reprice the risk itself.

At the plan level, PBMs guarantee pharmacy costs on paper while gaming exclusions and rebates, and stop-loss carriers quote based on historical experience that doesn't exist for new groups. The startups winning here aren't selling analytics alone but underwriting the risk directly. [[Sources/Portfolio/WriteWise/|WriteWise]] guarantees total pharmacy PMPM and profits from the spread between its predicted and actual cost. [[Sources/LLM-Chats/2025-12-21-AI-for-stop-loss-insurers|Gradient AI]] quotes groups with no claims history through data substitution, turning uninsurable risk into a priced product. At the provider level, ACOs accept financial risk for patient populations without actuarial infrastructure to price it — [[Sources/Browser-History/2026-01-27-sparx|Sparx]] built that infrastructure, now serves 20%+ of ACOs, unlocked $1B+ in risk-bearing capacity, and in one contract gave leadership enough confidence to take downside risk and realize 62.1% savings. At the individual level, a healthy 30-year-old overpays $6,000-7,000/year on an employer plan to cross-subsidize risk the insurer prices at the population, not the person — platforms that separate routine care from catastrophic risk reprice that individual's actual exposure. What connects these isn't the healthcare vertical — it's the underwriting position. Each company controls the pricing decision, takes on risk, and profits from the accuracy gap between its models and incumbent assumptions. The buyers are the entities closest to the risk and furthest from accurate pricing: TPAs (2,700+ in the US), MGUs, ACOs, and self-funded employers who need granular underwriting and can't get it from carriers built for population-level pooling. Gene therapy tail risk ($2-4.5M single treatments, only 3 claims across 2M covered lives 2022-2024) is the emerging extension — treatments like Lenmeldy ($4.25M) create catastrophic exposure that actuarial models can't price because frequency data doesn't exist yet, and whoever aggregates that data first builds the moat.

The risk is that incumbents close the gap — through brute-force repricing (Voya raised 21%+), acquisition of AI capability ([[Sources/Browser-History/2025-12-23-nationwide-to-acquire-allstate-employer-stop-loss|Nationwide's $1.25B purchase of Allstate's employer stop-loss business]]), or building in-house (UnitedHealth has 2,000+ AI engineers, launched [[Sources/Browser-History/2026-01-27-optum-real|Optum Real]] in October 2025). If a major carrier acquires a Gradient AI or builds equivalent underwriting infrastructure, the pricing advantage narrows at that layer. Regulatory risk compounds this: DOL/HHS/Treasury flagged concerns about stop-loss bypassing consumer protections, and New York, California, and Delaware already restrict stop-loss for small employers — federal action could constrain the self-funded growth feeding the plan-level opportunity. But the deeper bet is that this isn't a technology gap incumbents can close with engineers. It's a business model gap. Carriers profit from opacity and population-level pricing. An underwriting platform that prices at the group or individual level and passes savings through is structurally misaligned with how carriers make money — these startups don't compete with carriers, they make carrier pricing legible, and legibility is exactly what carriers have every incentive to prevent. That misalignment is what makes the arbitrage durable even as incumbents add AI capability.
