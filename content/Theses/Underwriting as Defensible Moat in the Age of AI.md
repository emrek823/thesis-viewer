# Underwriting as Defensible Moat in the Age of AI

**Overview**
<empty-block/>
We've seen a rise in interest around actuarial talent ([Arbital acquiring SBA](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/arbital-health-completes-acquisition-of-santa-barbara-actuaries-and-closes-series-a-financing-302025836.html)) and more companies being built to take advantage of imprecise underwriting from incumbents today. In our portfolio, [WriteWise](https://www.writewise.com/) reprices pharmacy claims at the individual drug level and guarantees employers a fixed PMPM. Outside the portfolio, [Sparx](https://usesparx.com/) underwrites ACO risk and has unlocked \$1B+ in reinsurance capacity by giving ACOs the actuarial confidence to take downside contracts.
The edge at these companies isn't necessarily an AI breakthrough. It's clever actuarial work, data aggregation earned through GTM focus (WriteWise assembled 100M claims across 20 PBMs because PBMs won't share that data with each other), and doing things incumbents are structurally unwilling and unable to do. AI helps with the data wrangling and math, but the real evolution with AI is that the rest of the stack (in WriteWise’s case dashboards for brokers , workflow tools for patient steerage according to formulary design, etc( is getting commoditized. That makes the underwriting layer, where you guarantee a number and bear the risk, more valuable by contrast.
**Why can't incumbents just do this themselves?** Three reasons, all structural:
1. **Opacity is the margin.** PBMs make money on spread pricing, which is the gap between what they charge the plan sponsor and what they pay the pharmacy. Carriers charge high-risk prices to low-risk groups and pocket the cross-subsidy. Pricing precisely would reveal the spread and eliminate it. They are rationally choosing to be imprecise.
2. **Distribution is built around opacity.** Brokers and consultants earn commissions as a percentage of premium. Transparent, lower pricing means lower commissions. The entire channel is incentivized to maintain the current structure.
3. **Capital and compliance assume population pooling.** Reserve calculations, regulatory filings (community rating, MLR floors), and reinsurance treaties are built around group-level risk. Unwinding that is a multi-year, multi-billion dollar project no board will approve when the current model still works.
The resulting spread is massive. WriteWise found a \$12.2M gap on a single employer group between what the incumbent PBM charged and the true cost. One Sparx contract realized 62.1% savings. UNH, CVS, and Centene collectively mispriced \~\$19B in medical costs in a single year. UNH has 2,000+ AI engineers. They have the technology but they don't have the incentive. 
What makes this a compounding advantage rather than a one-time arbitrage is the flywheel. Every claim and outcome feeds back into the pricing model. WriteWise gets more precise with every employer group, Sparx improves with every ACO performance year. In addition, there's a second loop we’ve seen emerge. Operational execution produces the outcomes the model predicted, which validates the model, which earns reinsurance capacity, which lets you take on more risk. To be specific, if you are underwriting  The companies that close this loop first build advantages that are nearly impossible to replicate.

## What This Means Going Forward
The winning recipe has three ingredients:
1. **Granular risk selection** — pricing at member-level or SKU-level resolution, not demographic buckets.
2. **Operational execution** that produces the outcome the model predicted — you can't just price risk, you have to deliver on the guarantee.
3. **A closed feedback loop** where outcomes train the model. The method varies — WriteWise uses actuarial repricing, Sparx uses predictive modeling, Indigo uses ML on physician histories — but the business model is the same: guarantee a number, bear the risk, capture the spread.
There are creative structures that make these businesses scalable and evolve into insurance plans. WriteWise has an asset-light MGU structure and doesn't hold risk on its balance sheet, doesn't own a pharmacy, and distributes through brokers and TPAs. Sparx unlocks capacity through surety bonds rather than letters of credit. Neither requires the capital base of a carrier. Both capture underwriting margin at software-like gross margins.
This playbook ports to any healthcare cost category where the unit of risk is standardizable, incumbent pricing is obfuscated, and variance can be modeled with data:
- **Surgical episodes** (\$400B) — Surgeries are priced on blunt CPT-code averages and regional benchmarks. A startup that selects patients, bundles the episode, and guarantees a price captures the spread between what the payer would have paid and what it actually costs. Hospitals profit from fee-for-service volume (opacity), referral networks are built around relationships not outcomes (distribution), and bundled payment infrastructure barely exists outside CMS pilots (capital/compliance). The underwriting edge is that post-op engagement (automated follow-ups, RPM, medication adherence) directly reduces complication variance, which tightens the guarantee.
- **Risk-bearing provider groups beyond ACOs** — Most provider groups want to take downside risk but lack the actuarial infrastructure to price it. CMS and payers set capitation using broad demographic adjusters; a startup with granular claims data can price at the provider-panel or member level. Payers profit from keeping providers on fee-for-service (opacity), and the groups themselves don't have actuarial talent (capital/compliance). The opportunity is giving MSOs, specialty groups, and risk-bearing IPAs the underwriting engine they need to take risk confidently. 
- **Stop-loss for self-funded employers** — Legacy carriers price on blunt group demographics (industry, size, geography). A startup pricing at the individual member level captures the spread between the group-level premium and the true risk. Carriers profit from blunt pricing (opacity), brokers are paid on premium volume (distribution), and reserves assume pooled risk (capital/compliance). Adjacent to what WriteWise already does for pharmacy.
- **Workers comp / disability** (\$55B and \$142B) — Actuarial models price on historical claim rates and industry codes, missing behavioral and temporal factors that drive the majority of outcomes. In disability, the arbitrage is temporal: return-to-work probability drops to \~50% after 6 months and below \~20% after 12, but legacy carriers don't intervene until month 6. A startup that identifies high-risk claims early and underwrites the cost of intervention captures the spread between what the claim would have cost and what it costs with intervention. Carriers profit from slow claims processing (opacity), distribution is locked into legacy brokers (distribution), and reserving models don't account for intervention ROI (capital/compliance).
