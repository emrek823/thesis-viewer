# XX 

“Data” is often generically talked about as an important moat in healthcare AI. 
Through our portfolio company [Integral](https://www.useintegral.com/), we’ve had a view from the infrastructure layer of how labs and human-data companies are thinking about post-training data. Outside of a few data modalities (medical imaging, antibody binding data), we haven’t seen many healthcare AI companies whose main business is selling data to frontier labs. But the lens of “what would a frontier lab actually pay for” turned out to be the sharpest forcing function we’ve found for thinking about data moats in healthcare AI — the work labs demand is the same work app-layer companies face when they try to use their own workflow data for training.
Because healthcare is more difficult to verify and a lower veracity domain, companies building in healthcare AI are moving towards real-world workflow data. In this world, activation, the combination of the right intelligence and the right expertise continuously delivered in a data pipeline, is what turns this workflow data into a moat. 
## Types of Post-training Data
A recent framework that resonated was [Sid Potdar's framework](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/data-labeling-other-euphemisms-sid-potdar-txdsc/) that splits post-training data into three categories: annotations on live model output, from-scratch synthetic environments, and real-world workflow data.

We see healthcare companies moving towards the 3rd because [verification is more difficult](https://www.jasonwei.net/blog/asymmetry-of-verification-and-verifiers-law) and it is a “[low-veracity domain](https://seanzcai.substack.com/p/an-ai-bubble-is-a-human-data-problem)”. Verifier asymmetry, or how hard it is to break a task into verifiable steps, is higher in healthcare because we don’t have free verifiers like GitHub provided for coding. <span discussion-urls="discussion://361c0b14-1fd6-80e9-92e9-001c10b73e64">You have to build verifiers from inside the workflow itself (clinician edits, payer adjudications, reimbursement outcomes) signals that exist nowhere else.</span> Healthcare is also a low-veracity domain where operational logic is heterogeneous and embedded. The workflow is the only place you can get these signals and this data.
To be more specific on what this data is, let’s take an example of EHR automation tasks for 2 different companies: one as system of record and one that is inserting into someone else’s workflow 
- **You are the system of record. Mostly inc**Next-gen EMRs like Canvas Medical are a good example here. Every encounter, prescription, and billing action on the platform runs through their software by default, so the corpus accumulates comprehensively and longitudinally without negotiation on data rights. The data captured is workflow exhaust do what actually happened across millions of clinical interactions. The expertise is extracting fit-for-AI signal from a corpus that wasn’t designed for AI training.
- **You insert into someone else’s workflow as a problem-solver.** [Kinetic Systems](https://www.kineticsystems.ai/) is a good example here. They go in to solve a specific operational problem (prior auth, DME, appeals) and by virtue of being inside customer workflows they surface the operational logic that governs those workflows. These include customer-specific business rules, payer-by-payer adjudication patterns, structured ontology, edge cases, and tribal knowledge. The data captured is the explicit logic of the workflow, generated as a byproduct of automating it. The surgical expertise is structured rule extraction; the moat is the rule corpus plus the in-workflow position that produced it.
Both modes capture genuinely different shapes of bucket 3 data. System-of-record captures workflow exhaust and what happened at scale. Workflow providers capture operational logic — the rules and structure behind why it happened that way. They are orthogonal cuts at the same underlying problem, and a frontier lab would pay for both. Both share the same downstream constraint: workflow access is necessary but not sufficient — the data still has to be refined before a lab will pay for it or your own training pipeline can use it.

## What we see going forward
**1. Training your own model becomes more common for app-layer companies.** Two years ago, fine-tuning your own model was viewed either as a fundraising tactic for claimed differentiation or as a poor allocation of resources, since frontier models would catch up by the time you shipped. [That’s shifting](https://x.com/oneill_c/status/2054604986269802579). App-layer companies are moving off frontier APIs onto specialized models trained against their own interaction data, because the reward signal for being good at any specific workflow lives inside that workflow’s product, and nowhere else.
**2. Standalone data assets become less valuable.** Healthcare is inherently multi-modal. Today, a few data types (namely imaging and bio wet lab data) support standalone businesses and there is a moment in time where labs are investing heavily in these assets, but companies getting off the ground today are preparing for a different future. 
**3. Small, purpose-built models become economically viable for niche use cases.** When the underlying data is already refined and signal-preserved, the cost of building very specific models for very specific purposes — a rare kidney disease affecting 100 patients, a single payer’s prior auth pattern, a niche specialty workflow — drops sharply. You can train smaller, more accurate models surgically because the data engineering is already done, not redone from scratch each time. Frontier-model APIs don’t pencil for these use cases; small specialized models built on activated data do.
